Save North Middletown Elementary School.

The district's best elementary school is the one it has marked to close. On July 15, 2026, a planning committee voted to reclassify North Middletown Elementary as transitional, the first step toward closing it. Part One

The money problem is real. Closing NMES does not solve it.

The district ran a General Fund deficit of $2.65 million last year before transfers, after $2.54 million the year before; two years running is what makes it structural. Reserves are falling by about $1.1 million a year. I do not dispute any of that. The question is whether closing a 128-student school fixes it. It does not come close. The district's unaudited FY2026 close, presented to the board on July 16, 2026, reports the year down only about $374,000, but that headline includes $1.32 million moved in from restricted capital funds and a $1.41 million June receipt the packet never identifies; set those aside and the gap sits between $1.7 million and $3.1 million, the same range as the audited years. I walk through every line of it further down.

What actually drove the deficit

Federal pandemic aid wound down, taking about $2.95 million a year with it, and attendance-based state funding fell with roughly 248 fewer funded students in average daily attendance once the pandemic hold-harmless ended. Meanwhile, costs the district controls grew fast: central-office administration rose 44.8 percent in two years.

A fair caution I print beside this number: Kentucky audits fold state pension allocations into these expense lines, so some share may be accounting rather than hiring. That is why the report asks for a position-by-position accounting instead of assuming the worst.

The tax picture the budget talk leaves out

Bourbon County Schools taxes real estate at 52.4 cents per $100, second lowest of nine area districts and roughly 13 cents below the statewide school average. The board also holds a no-recall option to raise revenue 4 percent a year. The math is simple and it does not bend: either spending comes down or revenue goes up. Standing idle is the one answer I rule out.

And the fourteen-year record settles how unusual this county's path is. KDE publishes every district's levied rates back to tax year 2012. Every neighboring district's levied rate is higher today than fourteen years ago: Bath up 72.3 percent, Scott up 38.9, Harrison up 34.2, Clark up 22.2, Fayette up 18.4, Paris Independent up 17.2, Nicholas up 12.2, Montgomery up 6.3. Bourbon County's is 5.4 percent lower, the only decline in the region, sliding from second highest of the nine in 2012 to seventh today, including a twelve-cent fall from 2018 to 2022, the exact years the drawdowns began. Two honesty notes: these are levied rates, and under HB 44 a rate can drift down while collections hold as assessments grow, so the chart nets each board's choices against its assessment growth; and Bourbon did take the 4 percent option in five of the last twelve years, per KDE's own levied-type file, but the other seven years took compensating or less. The neighbors that rose took the 4 percent seven, eight, and nine times.

The bonds: a different pot of money

Kentucky law walls building money off from operating money by default; in the ordinary course none of it can pay teachers. The one exception is a temporary flexibility the state budget act allows, and the district's own year-end packet shows it used that exception in June 2026 to move $1.32 million of capital money into the General Fund, a point I come back to below. The $6.055 million bond issued in 2024 flowed into the construction fund, where construction in progress grew from $3.65 million to $7.41 million in one year, and it is why the district's debt payments jump this year. Its purpose is on the public record: the state bond disclosure, approved by the legislature's Capital Projects and Bond Oversight Committee in June 2024, lists a high school roof replacement and a districtwide audio system. No bond on record names NMES. And closing a school creates no borrowing room: bonding capacity is built from restricted streams, a $100 per student state allotment and the nickel building tax, that do not grow when a school closes. The FY2024 audit already showed about $23.5 million in unused capacity as of June 30, 2024. Section 6 of the report walks through the statute and breaks out every component.

The $14 million plan, and the levers not on the table

At the July 15 planning meeting, the superintendent said the district must free up $800,000 to $1,000,000 a year in operating money to bond $14 million. That is a general fund bond: the payment would come from the budget that pays teachers, and at roughly $0.8 to $1.1 million a year it is about the same size as the school's claimed operating cost. Under this plan, money saved by a closure would go to debt service, so the General Fund deficit itself would not shrink.

The claimed savings cannot carry that bond anyway. Bond capacity is yearly savings multiplied over the life of the bonds. The district's own school-level spending reports, filed with the state every year since 2019, put NMES's full excess cost near $121,000 a year, which supports about $1.6 million of bonds, not $14 million. Even the two-tailed best case, $565,000 a year, supports about $7.3 million, and the median outcome, $91,000, supports about $1.2 million. Only the administration's own unpublished $900,000 figure reaches the target.

Three funding sources are available that do not involve a closure. The audit's $23.5 million in bonding potential, less the 2024 issue, leaves roughly $18 million of restricted capacity, and the district's own debt schedule shows payments falling from $1.58 million to about $400,000 a year as old bonds retire after 2030, room a standard wrap-around bond structure is designed to use. And a correction I make myself, because every number here answers to the documents: I previously described a recallable nickel as an untaken option. KDE's SEEK payment schedules show Bourbon already levies one, inside the existing rate, alongside the original facilities nickel. The community is already paying both. The board levied it on August 17, 2023, and the recall window closed with no petition, so this community has already said yes to a facilities tax, twice. What is genuinely new: the state match on that nickel is phasing in on KDE's own schedules, $82,866 in FY2025 and $276,246 a year at full value on the FY2027 schedule, additional restricted money that grows bonding capacity with no local action at all. And for scale, the other school district in this county shows what these same tools can do: KDE's current levied-rates file puts Paris Independent at 71.5 cents, including 17.4 cents of recallable building levies, while Bourbon sits at 52.4 cents with one. SFCC state offers add more. These sources raise amounts comparable to the plan's $14 million, so the decision is a choice among funding mechanisms, and it should be made with the fiscal agent's current bonding potential statement public. The model's Debt_Service tab runs a combined scenario, balancing the operating budget first from levy increments and cost reductions, then funding construction from the remainder plus the new nickel equalization: roughly $21 million of total capacity in the conservative case and about $25 million on the FY2026 trend, with the capital-to-operations sweep ended in both cases so the building-fund stream is free to pledge. On the trend the budget balances with about $283,000 to spare; the conservative case remains about $1.24 million short, and its total respects that by leaning only on equalization and existing restricted room.

The year-end packet also answers a question I could not answer before: what "free up operating money to bond" actually means. In June 2026 the district moved $1,320,939 of restricted capital money into the General Fund, $1,098,663 from the Building Fund and $222,276 from Capital Outlay, which ended the year at exactly zero. (One bookkeeping note: the packet's sending-fund page records the Building Fund piece as $1,098,633, thirty dollars below its own General Fund ledger; we use the ledger figure, which sums exactly to the packet's printed total.) The transfer is lawful, under a flexibility provision Kentucky's budget acts have carried since 2020. The Building Fund piece was in the district's own budget from September 2025; the $222,276 Capital Outlay piece appears in no version of the FY2026 budget. Here is what it means for the bond plan. The Building Fund collects about $2.5 million a year from the two facilities nickels; debt service takes about $1.38 million; the roughly $1.1 million left over is precisely the restricted stream that could carry a $14 million bond. Right now that stream is plugging the operating budget instead. So the plan, decoded from the district's own books: make the General Fund stand on its own so the sweep can stop and the leftover nickel money can back the bond. Any million dollars a year does that job. The untaken 4 percent levy does it. The alternatives package does it. Cuts elsewhere in a $22 million budget do it. Closing the county's highest-scoring school is one option among several, and the smallest.

Now the unaudited FY2026 close itself, because I have read every line of the packet, including the district's KDE Budget Monitoring Tool. The headline is the best in three years: the General Fund down about $374,000, after audited drawdowns of $1.07 million and $1.23 million. The packet also shows how that headline was built. Two entries landed in June, the final month of the year: the $1,320,939 capital transfer above, and a $1,413,929 receipt booked to miscellaneous revenue against a budget of zero, in a line that had produced about $154,000 all the other eleven months combined. June revenue was $3.9 million against $1.1 million the June before, and those two entries are 98 percent of the difference. Set the capital transfer aside and the year is down about $1.7 million. Set the unidentified June receipt aside too and it is down about $3.1 million. Some things genuinely improved: SEEK funding came in $402,000 over budget, and salaries ran $588,000 under budget and $224,000 below last year. But the underlying gap sits in the same range it has been, and no one should build a case on the $374,000, in either direction, until the district identifies that June receipt, on the record. That identification is at the top of our questions for the board.

Room to grow: enrollment since 1989

The building held 261 children at its peak. It holds 128 today against a rated capacity of 174, a rating the district's own plans put at 198 as recently as 2013. The seats are there. Filled to capacity, who is the cheapest school? Depends which of the district's own capacity tables you believe

The district has filed spending for every school with the state since 2019, and five years of its own filings tell one story: NMES's total spending grew the least of the three elementaries, up 16 percent since 2019-20 against 37 percent at Bourbon Central and 47 percent at Cane Ridge. What grew is the empty space around each student. So I ran the fair test both ways: every school filled to its rated capacity, all three schools under the same rules, with variable costs priced honestly (supplies for every added or removed student, plus a full $85,000 teacher wherever class-size caps require another section) and validated against actuals: even pricing every added student at $9,848, the actual per-student cost difference between Bourbon Central and Cane Ridge on the same filing, a filled NMES lands at $16,837, below both receiving schools, and the supplies and staffed cases land lower still, $14,339 and $15,316 at the approved 174 rating, $16,149 and $16,701 at the draft's 154. In the pandemic-aid years a filled NMES would have run within 1 to 3 percent of the other two, a tie; in today's cost structure it is not close. And the symmetry runs both ways, because cost per student is a utilization measure, not a verdict on a building: move students out of the receiving schools and their per-student cost rises the same way.

Then run it across every capacity table the district itself has produced, and watch the answer change. Under the state-approved 2013 plan (NMES rated 198), the state-approved 2021 plan still in force (174), each school's own twenty-year peak enrollment, and the district's own architect's 2026 KFICS capacities, a filled NMES is the cheapest school in the county. Under the 2017 plan and the unapproved 2026 draft table, Bourbon Central is. Here is why that is the finding: the same three unchanged buildings have been rated 198, 152, 174, 154 (NMES), 564, 611, 521, 640 (Bourbon Central), and 500, 550, 422, 547 (Cane Ridge) across four consecutive plans, swings of up to 128 seats with no major construction after 2009. The two 2026 documents disagree with each other by 141 and 150 seats on the same two schools in the same cycle. And the recovered 2017 plan, corroborated by the state board's own June 7, 2017 minutes, rated NMES at 152 seats with 154 enrolled: the school now called half empty was listed OVER capacity nine years ago. A rating that moves that much is a policy choice, and whichever school the chosen table shortchanges will look expensive at capacity. That is the entire cost case against this school.

The 300-student breakeven, reconstructed to the dollar

At the last committee meeting, a figure was cited: NMES needs about 300 students to break even. No worksheet has been published with it, so I reconstructed it from the state's own files, and both sides of the fraction can now be named exactly. The cost side is $2,476,544, which is 128 students times the $19,348 per-student total on the 2023-24 state report card, to the dollar: a figure that includes federal spending and the state's on-behalf pension payments. The revenue side that yields 300 is state revenue alone: in 2022-23, the report-card year available when such a figure would have been built, state sources came to $8,305 per member, and $2,476,544 divided by $8,305 gives 298 students. No other revenue definition in the state's files lands near 300. Here is what the district actually collects per student, from KDE's own funding files:

YearLocalStateFederalTotal per member
2019-20$3,598$6,904$2,127$12,629
2022-23$4,812$8,305$3,892$17,009
2023-24$5,475$7,128$3,380$15,983

So the construction charges the school every dollar it spends from all three sources, then credits it only the state share of what its students bring in. The local and federal revenue those same 128 children generate, $8,855 per student in 2023-24, more than the state share, is counted on the cost side and skipped on the revenue side. Run the same fraction with matching definitions and the breakeven is 155 students, not 300. And here is the test that ends the argument: apply the 300-breakeven's own method, honestly corrected, to every school the district owns, and every one fails, including both receiving schools. Cane Ridge falls short by 76 students and $1.2 million a year on this test, nearly three times NMES's shortfall. Statewide, 99.6 percent of Kentucky's schools fail it. A test that fails every school in the district, and virtually every school in Kentucky, is not measuring schools. It is measuring the fact that a district in a drawdown year spends more per student than it collects, at every building equally.

The real breakeven question is: at what enrollment do a school's students bring in more than closing it could actually save? Fixed site base (principal, office, plant) against the funding a student carries: NMES clears that bar at 38 to 69 students and enrolls 128, roughly double to triple its true breakeven. The same test puts Cane Ridge's bar at 43 to 77 and Bourbon Central's at 48 to 86; every school clears comfortably, which is how school finance is supposed to work. The full grid version published in Part Three, with position, busing, and leaver effects, brackets NMES's bar at 20 to 122 students. Every construction of the question except one, all-source costs divided by state-only revenue, says this school pays its way. I ask one thing of the 300 figure: publish the worksheet. If it differs from this reconstruction, its own records are the fastest way to show it.

Building condition, as reported to the state: the district's best-trending building

Kentucky's facilities inventory system (KFICS) gives every public school building a Condition Index: one minus the ratio of repairs due within four years to the cost of replacing the building, so higher is healthier. The state has published exactly three statewide reports, and here is every number it has ever printed for Bourbon County's elementary schools. In the newest, KDE-reviewed data (fresh April 2026 inspections), NMES posts 0.773: better than Cane Ridge (0.728), within six points of Bourbon Central (0.823), and far ahead of the middle school (0.596). NMES is the only school in the district whose condition improved between the 2020-21 and April 2026 inspection cycles, and its four-year repair bill, $3.1 million, is the smallest of the district's five schools. Cane Ridge, a 1992 building, went the other way: 0.812 down to 0.728, with a $4.8 million bill. One caveat, on the record: NMES's separate Educational Suitability score prints 0.21725 in the July 2026 report, identical to five decimal places to 2023, so that half of the aggregate appears carried forward rather than re-surveyed. The building proposed for closure is, on the state's own newest data, the healthiest small asset the district owns.

Millersburg: this county has run the play before

Millersburg Elementary, about nine road miles from Paris, closed in 2006 with 119 students, nearly the size of NMES today. Its children went to Cane Ridge, whose addition the district's own facility plan dates to 2007. The census tells the rest: Millersburg fell from 842 people in 2000 to 792 in 2010 to 747 in 2020, down 11 percent, while the county grew 4.6 percent. No one blames the school closure alone, and neither do we, and we are careful not to confuse the town's two lost schools: the private Millersburg Military Institute, wounded by a temporary closure in 2003, shut for good in July 2006, and the public elementary never opened that fall, per its federal closure record. Joy Global then left in 2013 with 197 jobs and, by the town's own accounting, half its budget. That is the honest lesson, and it is worse: small towns lose their anchors in cascades, and every departure hands the remaining families one more reason to follow. Twenty years later, the school that absorbed Millersburg's children is over its rated capacity, and the proposed fix is to close another small school. NMES's attendance zone holds 2,625 people. The records request list now includes the savings analysis from the 2006 closure and the savings actually realized, so this community can compare projection to outcome before the same projection is made twice.

Has this ever worked in Kentucky? We checked all thirty years

From the federal record of every Kentucky public school since 1994, we built the complete list: 339 rural and small-town school closures since 1995, including 72 towns that lost their last public school (Millersburg is one, which validates the method). Then we priced all 163 closures with clean finance data per displaced student, crediting each district's entire budget gap against the state trend to its closure, the most generous possible reading, and published the whole distribution below, nothing hidden. The median closure produced $1,102 per displaced student ($818 among physically plausible cases), and 40 percent of districts spent MORE than trend after closing. Both tails hold events beyond $13,000 per child, more than any school even costs to run per student, in both directions: proof those gaps are budget-wide noise, not closure savings, and why our own estimate is built bottom-up from positions, busing, and state funding instead. One number published for transparency because anyone can compute it from our spreadsheet: the raw median for the 27 closures most like this plan prints $8,440, an artifact of dividing district-wide budget noise by 60-to-320-student schools (11 of the 27 exceed the physical ceiling); inside the plausible window that median is $541. The yardstick: this plan needs $6,250 to $7,813 per displaced child, above the record's 75th percentile. Among rural elementary closures, the one clean no-construction comparable, Webster County's Slaughters Elementary in 2012, paid $3,525; every case at or near the plan's number built a brand-new school (Perry 2017, Adair 2006, Metcalfe 2013). And the convergence is the point: the record's plausible median is $818; our own bottom-up model, built without ever looking at that history, says $713. Two independent methods, one answer, and the plan needs nine times it.

The classroom finding: of 42 closures measurable within one state testing system, 11 improved against the state, 10 declined, 21 were flat, and the more children a closure displaced, the worse the trend (closures moving 15 percent or more of a district ran a median 2 points down). Exactly one event shows both clear savings and clear gains: Leslie County 2013, a middle school folded into an existing campus in the same community. The two best cases for a closure advocate, disclosed here so no one has to dig for them: Perry County (real but modest savings, bought with new construction, scores continuing a climb that began years earlier) and Johnson County 2016 (a 178-student rural elementary closed, spending 6 points under trend, scores flat: savings without improvement, in proportion to the school's 5 percent share). No case exists of a Kentucky district closing a rural town's elementary school, clearly saving money, and clearly improving scores. If the board believes it will be the first in thirty years, show us the data.

None of this is a recommendation of a particular number, and none of it is counted in the alternatives package below. The point is narrower: a menu of options exists between cut nothing and close a school, every one prices out larger than the most generous closure estimate, and every one carries a built-in democratic check. A levy above 4 percent can be recalled by the voters it taxes. A closed school cannot be recalled by the children it displaces. This community was offered that veto twice on the facilities nickels and twice declined to use it. It has never been offered the same vote on the operating levy that pays teachers.

The fill-the-seats planner

Rebalance attendance boundaries, recruit cross-county transfers, and win back homeschool and private-school families until NMES reaches its rated 174. Each transfer or returning student brings the state's $4,626 per-student funding at the fiscal 2027 base; Section 9 of the report shows the full math. The recruits are not hypothetical, and the pool is measured: the two districts' own records show 259 registered homeschool students (2022-23), the Census puts about one in three county school-age kids in private school or homeschooling (up from about one in eight before the pandemic), and KDE's own nonresident report shows 131 out-of-county students already enrolled in Bourbon County schools, 54 of them from Fayette County. Families already drive into this district; filling NMES's 46 open seats takes fewer than one in five of the registered homeschoolers alone.

The map the rebalancing plan stands on

Half the county's people live in Paris, where both receiving schools sit. The growth path: a district-wide recovery plan, fully priced

Name the problem correctly and the plan writes itself. Bourbon County Schools has a structural funding problem: spending has outrun revenue at every building, salaries are hard to raise, and capital projects wait, and NMES is being blamed for it. The numbers on this page say otherwise. NMES is not the reason salaries cannot increase; its honest excess cost is about $156,000 a year, six tenths of one percent of the budget. NMES is not the reason capital projects cannot be funded; the district holds roughly $17.6 million of unused restricted bonding capacity, and the sweep that drains the building fund into operations is a General Fund problem every school shares. A district in a defensive crouch, cutting a rural community's school to buy months, is choosing the smallest lever on the table and the only one that harms a town. Road Ahead

A school is not closed by a single vote. Under Kentucky's facility-planning rules, it takes a chain of public meetings and separate votes by the planning committee, the elected school board, and the Kentucky Board of Education. Most of those steps are still ahead of us. What makes a school "transitional"

Separate from the steps above, Kentucky keeps a school as a permanent center when it meets four of six criteria. Meet fewer than four, and it can be labeled transitional. Here are the six, and where North Middletown stands today.

  • 1. Test scores. Meets the state's curriculum threshold in one of the last two testing cycles. NMES leads the county, it meets this.
  • 2. Bus rides. Under 45 minutes for most young students. As a neighborhood school, NMES meets this today. Closure is what breaks it.
  • 3. Cost per student. Within 150 percent of the district's lowest-cost like school. NMES is about 107 percent of the lowest, it meets this.
  • 4. Equal programs. Offers 90 percent of what similar schools offer.
  • 5. Building condition. At least 30 years of life left, with renovation under 80 percent of replacement cost.
  • 6. Community support. Volunteer hours, PTA/PTO membership, an outside sponsor, and a community service program.
Closing North Middletown would be the board's choice, not a rule it has to follow. Even when a school meets these criteria, Kentucky lets the board apply the transitional label anyway, through a separate, discretionary path. Meeting the state's own standard for a permanent school is not an automatic shield. So the fair question to put to the district, in writing, is simple: why label this school transitional when it meets that standard? Ask for their written scoring on all six criteria.
