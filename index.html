Part One

The District Needs Growth, Not Closures

Bourbon County Schools does not have a North Middletown problem. It has a district-wide problem: enrollment falling while the county's children stay put, fixed costs rising at every building, and a revenue posture no neighbor shares. Every piece of it is fixable, and none of the fixes involve closing a school. The problem first, then the three levers: grow enrollment, trim fixed costs, and face the rate question the way every neighboring board already has.

Part One

The money problem is real. Closing NMES does not solve it.

The district ran a General Fund deficit of $2.65 million last year before transfers, after $2.54 million the year before; two years running is what makes it structural. Reserves are falling by about $1.1 million a year. I do not dispute any of that. The question is whether closing a 128-student school fixes it. It does not come close. The district's unaudited FY2026 close, presented to the board on July 16, 2026, reports the year down only about $374,000, but that headline includes $1.32 million moved in from restricted capital funds and a $1.41 million June receipt the packet never identifies; set those aside and the gap sits between $1.7 million and $3.1 million, the same range as the audited years. The report walks through every line of it, along with the bond plan and the June capital transfer.

What actually drove the deficit

Federal pandemic aid wound down, taking about $2.95 million a year with it, and attendance-based state funding fell with roughly 248 fewer funded students in average daily attendance once the pandemic hold-harmless ended. Meanwhile, costs the district controls grew fast: central-office administration rose 44.8 percent in two years. Section 2 of the report decomposes that increase line by line from the district's own working budgets, and the honest finding is that administrative salaries actually fell: the growth is insurance, statutory collection fees, retirement sick-leave payouts and professional services.

A fair caution I print beside this number: Kentucky audits fold state pension allocations into these expense lines, so some share may be accounting rather than hiring. That is why the report asks for a position-by-position accounting instead of assuming the worst.

Statement of Activities, both audits

The children never left the county. The district lost them.

The case for closing a school starts with a premise: there are fewer children. I went and checked, using exact census counts rather than projections, and the premise is false. What changed is not how many children live here. It is how many of them go to a Bourbon County public school.

Start with the hard number. The decennial census counts every child, it does not sample them. Children aged 5 to 17 in Bourbon County: 3,594 in 2000, 3,574 in 2010, 3,548 in 2020. Twenty-five years, a change of 1.3 percent. The Census Bureau's separate annual school-age estimates agree, moving from about 3,400 in 2014 to about 3,491 in 2023. There is no demographic decline story here to tell.

Now the enrollment. Federal fall counts, not estimates: the two county districts together held 3,708 students in fall 2019 and 3,428 in fall 2022. Bourbon County Schools alone went from 2,912 in 2014 to 2,616 in 2023, down 10.2 percent, while the county's children moved four tenths of one percent. The elementary grades fell 16.5 percent from their 2016 peak, 1,245 to 1,040. The break is not gradual and it is not a birth-rate story: it begins after 2019.

So where are they? Reconciling the district rosters, the Census, and the state's own non-resident file for 2024-25 against one consistent frame, the answer is about 450 to 550 children, roughly 13 to 15 percent of the county's school-age population, in private school or homeschooling. A decade ago that share was around one in eleven. The ground-truth count reaches most of the way there on its own: 259 registered homeschool students in 2022-23, up from 170 in 2018-19, plus roughly 100 at Bourbon Christian Academy and about 60 at St. Mary, which is a floor because it misses families who commute to Lexington. At the state's $4,226 net per student, those children represent about $2.1 to $2.3 million a year in funding that walks past both county districts.

Growth Lever One

Build enrollment. Every returning student is nearly pure margin.

One more child in an existing classroom brings the state's $4,626 SEEK base against roughly $400 of supplies. The district is losing that trade 25 times a year at the front door: kindergarten intake hit 149 last fall, the lowest in the federal record, and fewer children have entered kindergarten than left fifth grade in seven of the last eight years. The pool to win back is measured, not imagined: 259 registered homeschool students, roughly 450 to 550 county children in private or home settings, and 131 out-of-county students already choosing these schools.

Differentiate the offering

The proof this works is already on the books in Kentucky. Eminence Independent, a small K-12 district in Henry County about an hour from Bourbon County, rebuilt itself around a distinctive project-based program and open nonresident enrollment: federal enrollment data show it growing from 733 students in 2014 to 1,006 in 2024, up 37 percent, over the same decade Bourbon County shrank 13 percent. Beechwood Independent grew 20 percent on the same open-market forces. Growth is a choice districts have made, in this state, at this scale, under these laws.

Themed programs cost little

A themed academy converts the district's smallest school into its most distinctive offering, and the theme is a community choice with three natural fits. An arts academy: arts integration is the lowest-cost differentiation there is, and the magnet model is one Kentucky's larger districts have run for decades. A technology academy: the building blocks are already free, because the school qualifies for the Amazon Future Engineer computer science program three separate ways (rural NCES locale, 76 percent free and reduced lunch, Title I). An agriculture and outdoor sciences academy: matched to the county's economy and the school's 110-square-mile rural zone. Under any theme, the 21st Century Community Learning Centers grant funds exactly the after-school care young families need, and a full-day preschool-to-kindergarten pipeline uses a funnel the district already owns: its preschool runs over capacity in both facility plans.

Scholarships and incentives, done lawfully

Kentucky's constitution bars paying students to enroll from public funds. It does not bar a community foundation from funding scholarship promises with private donations: a named college scholarship for students who complete K-5 at a district school, funded by donors and businesses, is the lawful version of an enrollment incentive, and promise-style programs are a proven draw. Paired with fee waivers, free after-school care, and transportation for nonresident transfers, the district can offer families real value that private options cannot match.

Room to grow: enrollment since 1989

The building held 261 children at its peak. It holds 128 today against a rated capacity of 174, a rating the district's own plans put at 198 as recently as 2013. The seats are there. The answer is to fill them and grow NMES into the strongest elementary school in the region. A specialized program for advanced learners, the magnet model Kentucky's larger districts have run for decades, is one natural path; a preschool satellite or simply growing the neighborhood school are others, and every child in the zone keeps their seat under all of them. The fill-the-seats planner

Rebalance attendance boundaries, recruit cross-county transfers, and win back homeschool and private-school families until NMES reaches its rated 174. Each transfer or returning student brings the state's $4,626 per-student funding at the fiscal 2027 base; Section 9 of the report shows the full math. The recruits are not hypothetical, and the pool is measured: the two districts' own records show 259 registered homeschool students (2022-23), reconciling rosters, census counts and the state's non-resident file puts about 450 to 550 county school-age kids, 13 to 15 percent, in private school or homeschooling, up from around one in eleven a decade ago (an earlier version of this page said one in three, from the Census survey alone; the rosters cap it lower and the corrected figure is above), and KDE's own nonresident report shows 131 out-of-county students already enrolled in Bourbon County schools, 54 of them from Fayette County. Families already drive into this district; filling NMES's 46 open seats takes fewer than one in five of the registered homeschoolers alone.

The state's frozen transportation budget reimburses nothing on new miles, which is closure's problem, not this plan's. That is the virtual competition already collecting this district's SEEK money, and a school with room is the counteroffer.

The tax picture the budget talk leaves out

Bourbon County Schools taxes real estate at 52.4 cents per $100, second lowest of nine area districts and roughly 13 cents below the statewide school average. The board also holds a no-recall option to raise revenue 4 percent a year. The math is simple and it does not bend: either spending comes down or revenue goes up. Standing idle is the one answer I rule out.

And the fourteen-year record settles how unusual this county's path is. KDE publishes every district's levied rates back to tax year 2012. Every neighboring district's levied rate is higher today than fourteen years ago: Bath up 72.3 percent, Scott up 38.9, Harrison up 34.2, Clark up 22.2, Fayette up 18.4, Paris Independent up 17.2, Nicholas up 12.2, Montgomery up 6.3. Bourbon County's is 5.4 percent lower, the only decline in the region, sliding from second highest of the nine in 2012 to seventh today, including a twelve-cent fall from 2018 to 2022, the exact years the drawdowns began. Two honesty notes: these are levied rates, and under HB 44 a rate can drift down while collections hold as assessments grow, so the chart nets each board's choices against its assessment growth; and Bourbon did take the 4 percent option in five of the last twelve years, per KDE's own levied-type file, but the other seven years took compensating or less. The neighbors that rose took the 4 percent seven, eight, and nine times.

What the recall record actually shows

The fear that stops every rate conversation is the recall petition. Three facts size that fear honestly. First, the 4 percent revenue option can never be recalled, and compounding it is how the neighbors built their advantage: Bourbon took it in 5 of the last 12 years while the neighbors that passed it took it 7, 8 and 9 times. No neighboring district climbed past Bourbon through above-4-percent leaps; they compounded the safe option. Second, where Kentucky communities have voted on recallable school taxes, the record is mixed and survivable: Marion County voters upheld their board's facilities nickel 54 to 46 in 2015, Christian County voters rejected one two-to-one in 2019, Bath County lost a vote and then re-levied. Recall fights are real, and boards win them when the plan behind the tax is concrete. Third, and closest to home: Bourbon County's own voters have been offered the recall twice, on the 2005-era and 2023 facilities nickels, and never filed a petition either time. This community's actual, demonstrated appetite for school taxes is higher than its board's. And one document settles what any board actually did in any year: the certified compensating rate against the rate levied, year by year, which remains on the open records list in the report.

One more lever no one has named: eight Kentucky districts levy an occupational license tax under KRS 160.605, including Scott County at 0.5 percent. Bourbon levies none. The utility tax, by contrast, is already at its 3 percent maximum, and the board deserves credit for that. The full mechanics of rates, the compensating rate, and House Bill 44 are worked through just below and in Section 11 of the report.

State law lets the board raise property-tax revenue 4 percent a year from existing property, and voters cannot petition to roll it back. It compounds. Slide the years.

The 4 percent is a limit on revenue, not on the rate

This distinction decides an argument that comes up at every forum, so it is worth setting out plainly. The district says it takes the 4 percent option. The published rate has not gone up. Both of those can be true at the same time, and usually are. Under KRS 160.470 the board's benchmark each August is not last year's rate. It is the compensating rate, defined as the rate that raises the same dollars from existing property as the year before. When assessments rise, that rate falls automatically, by exactly the amount assessments rose. The 4 percent option is the compensating rate multiplied by 1.04. So a board taking the full 4 percent every year, in a county whose property is appreciating, publishes a falling rate and collects more money. Here is that math on Bourbon's own figures, the fiscal 2025 certified assessment of $1,843,569,625 and the 41.0 General Fund cents in KDE's levied-rates file:

That is why a flat or falling rate is not evidence either way, and why it is a mistake to argue about it. It is also why raising the rate 4 percent is a different act entirely: last year's rate times 1.04, against 5 percent assessment growth, raises about 9.2 percent more revenue, and every dollar above the 4 percent ceiling can be petitioned to the ballot. The naive rate overshoots the protected rate by exactly the assessment growth rate, every time.

So what does the nine-district chart measure? Not property markets. Every one of these nine boards sets its rate each August against its own compensating rate, computed from its own assessment growth. Assessment growth is already netted out before any of them votes. What is left is the choice. Eight of the nine levy a higher rate today than in 2012; Bourbon's is 5.4 percent lower, the only decline in the region, and it has fallen from second highest of the nine to seventh. KDE's levied-type file shows Bourbon took the 4 percent option in five of the last twelve years, against seven, eight and nine times for the neighbors that rose. So the honest framing of the revenue question is not that this county's assessments went up; they went up everywhere. It is that eight boards facing the same mechanism ended up with more resources to compete with, and one did not. One document settles the rest of it in a single line, and it has been requested: for each of the last five years, the certified compensating rate against the rate the board actually levied. If the 4 percent was taken, the levied rate sits 4 percent above the compensating rate. Every year. On the page.

One movement in the chart needs explaining, because it cuts both ways. Bourbon's total levied rate rose 3.2 cents in 2023-24, from 49.2 to 52.4, and has held there for three years. That increase was not operating money. KDE's Nickel Levy Chart dates the board's recallable facilities nickel to August 17, 2023, and KDE's current levied-rates file puts that nickel at 5.7 cents. A new 5.7-cent restricted levy inside a 3.2-cent net increase means the rest of the rate came down about 2.5 cents in the same year, roughly $477,000 a year of unrestricted revenue at Bourbon's own $191,000 per cent. Two caveats, and they matter: KDE does not publish the year-by-year rate-type split, so the 2.5 cents is an inference from the current file and not a document, and a facilities nickel is an equivalent rate that is restated annually and drifts. The certified split by year is on the open records list below for exactly this reason. What is not in doubt is the direction. The levy that went up in 2023 was money that cannot lawfully pay a teacher.

Beyond the 4 percent: the recallable levy options

The 4 percent option is the largest increase the board can take without offering voters a veto. It is not the ceiling. Kentucky law (KRS 160.470) lets the board levy any rate, with the portion above 4 percent subject to a voter recall petition, and that is exactly how every neighboring district in the chart above climbed past Bourbon. Each option below is priced at Bourbon's own audited yield of about $191,000 per cent of rate ($7,829,060 of General Fund collections across the 41.0 General Fund cents in KDE's levied-rates file), real estate only, so the figures are conservative. What it unlocks. The first call on any new recurring money is closing the operating gap ($373,989 on the FY2026 trend) and ending the $1,320,939 capital-to-General-Fund sweep, $1,694,928 in all, because ending the sweep is what frees the restricted building stream to carry bonds. Restoring the 2018 rate raises $1,699,479, covering that requirement to within $4,551. The rate this board itself levied eight years ago is, almost to the dollar, the make-the-General-Fund-stand-alone rate. Once the General Fund stands alone, the nickel residual carries the $14 million renovation, the roughly $17.6 million of unused restricted capacity becomes genuinely pledgeable, and the phasing-in nickel equalization adds about $3.6 million more: roughly $35 million of construction capacity, for $15.69 a month on the median home, without pledging a cent of the new levy to a bond and without closing anything. Pledged straight to construction instead, the four options carry about $13.2, $19.6, $22.1, and $32.5 million at the model's 4.5 percent, 20-year assumption. The Harrison and median options are honest partial steps; they leave about $680,000 and $190,000 a year still to find from the alternatives menu before the sweep can end. None of this is a recommendation of a particular number, and none of it is counted in the alternatives package below. The point is narrower: a menu of options exists between cut nothing and close a school, every one prices out larger than the most generous closure estimate, and every one carries a built-in democratic check. A levy above 4 percent can be recalled by the voters it taxes. A closed school cannot be recalled by the children it displaces. This community was offered that veto twice on the facilities nickels and twice declined to use it. It has never been offered the same vote on the operating levy that pays teachers.

The growth path: a district-wide recovery plan, fully priced

Name the problem correctly and the plan writes itself. Bourbon County Schools has a structural funding problem: spending has outrun revenue at every building, salaries are hard to raise, and capital projects wait, and NMES is being blamed for it. The numbers on this page say otherwise. NMES is not the reason salaries cannot increase; its honest excess cost is about $156,000 a year against the cheaper receiving school ($121,220 on the district's own KDE-filed comparison to the peer average), six tenths of one percent of the budget. NMES is not the reason capital projects cannot be funded; the district holds roughly $17.6 million of unused restricted bonding capacity, and the sweep that drains the building fund into operations is a General Fund problem every school shares. A district in a defensive crouch, cutting a rural community's school to buy months, is choosing the smallest lever on the table and the only one that harms a town. What closure risks: the revenue loss vectors, priced

Three ways a closure loses money. First, students who leave the district rather than change schools. Every exit is free and funded under today's rules: HB 563 makes a transfer to any neighboring district free to the family and funded for the district that wins the child, registered homeschooling in this county has climbed from 170 to 259 in five years, and the statewide virtual academy, which did not exist three years ago, grew from 937 to 2,412 students in its first two years. The 300-student breakeven, and every version of the cost case against this school, rests on $2,476,544. That figure comes from the federal per-student report, which spreads district-wide costs across buildings. The district's own "Overall Cost by ORG" summary, produced in response to an open records request, codes $1,285,310 to North Middletown. Measured, school administration plus custodial plus plant is $227,831, within half a percent of the lower figure, and every fixed line at the school including the library totals $276,928, which is less than the $290,000 I published as the high case. My estimate was good, and slightly generous to the closure at the top.

Second, and this is the part that cuts hardest, so I have acted on it rather than noted it: my old $230,000 was the floor of the model, not a floor case. Reaching it required that the principal, the secretary, the custodian and the utilities all go. Districts frequently redeploy people rather than cut them: a principal takes an assistant principal post, a secretary moves to another office, a custodian moves to another building. If that happens here, the closure avoids the utilities and nothing else, $58,774. The old grid contained no such scenario, which meant every number I published assumed the district would make the deepest cut available to it.

So I rebuilt the grid. The fixed-cost lever now carries the three decisions the district can actually make with these lines, all measured rather than estimated: staff reassigned and utilities only, $58,774; building mothballed with those positions cut, $227,831; building sold, adding the library and media line, $276,928. That takes the grid from 1,944 combinations to 2,916. It is the single largest correction in this release and it moves my own headline against me: the median saving falls from $91,000 to $21,571, the share of scenarios that lose money rises from 29 percent to 45 percent, and the range widens to minus $556,006 and plus $551,928. One question to the district would narrow all of it: which of these positions would be eliminated, and which reassigned?

There is a limit to what any ledger can settle, and it is worth naming. The seven inputs that decide a closure's net effect span about $950,000. The district's books can speak to two of them, worth about a third of that spread. The rest, how many families leave, whether the building is sold or mothballed, whether the receiving schools need capacity work, how far the buses go, and whether staff are cut or moved, are decisions, not accounting. The range is wide because the decisions are open.

The Decision

Two roads: shrink to fit, or grow and thrive.

The board and the planning committee face one real choice, and it is not about one building. Shrink to fit: close the county's highest-scoring school for a median $21,571 a year, accept the leakage risk the district's own 2006 closure demonstrated, and tell every family watching that this district answers decline with retreat. Or grow and thrive: fill the seats, trim the fixed costs that actually move the budget, face the rate question every neighbor has faced, and compete for the families this county never lost.

On North Middletown, Specifically

Four asks that cost the district nothing.

1. Keep the school Permanent in the facility plan

List its capital needs at a lower priority. A facility plan commits no money: the regulation itself says plans are implemented "to the extent that the financial ability of the district permits," and Priority 2 exists precisely for acknowledged needs that are not yet funded. The alternative label, Transitional, saves nothing and forecloses everything: it removes the school from state facilities funding eligibility, blocks major renovation, and under the state manual's own definition declares a phase-out decided. Permanent at low priority and Transitional cost the same this year: zero. Only one of them keeps every door open.

2. Be honest that near-term savings are minimal

If staff are retained and reassigned rather than eliminated, the most conservative case in the closure grid and the pattern small-district closures typically follow, the closure frees the utilities line: $58,774 a year, about a quarter of one percent of the budget. The restricted renovation money is capped by enrollment anyway: at today's enrollment the state formula limits restricted-fund renovation to roughly a third of replacement cost, a ceiling that rises with every student recruited back. There is no pot of building money a closure unlocks.

3. Give the school the four years to the next plan

The facility plan runs on a four-year cycle. Let the community and the school run a real enrollment push, the themed program, the preschool funnel, the nonresident recruitment, the foundation scholarships, and measure it. If enrollment recovers toward the rated 174, the school's finances, its renovation ceiling, and its state funding all recover with it. If the push fails on a fair trial, the next planning cycle can revisit the question with actual evidence instead of projections.

4. Let the community carry the building's critical needs

North Middletown has already shown it will fight for this school; let it fund and build for it too. A community fundraising drive and donated services, contractors, materials, labor, for the building's genuinely critical needs costs the district nothing and is how small Kentucky towns have always kept their schools standing. The district's role is one sentence in a board motion: accept the help.
