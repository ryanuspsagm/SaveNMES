North Middletown, Kentucky • Est. 1948 • Home of the Kings

Save North Middletown Elementary School.

On July 15, 2026, a planning committee voted to label NMES "transitional." That is step one toward closing it. We did a deep dive into the public records, and filed Open Records Requests, to analyze this decision and better inform the Board of Education. You can check every number below.

Key Points

The case against closing NMES is clear. It has the county's best test scores. It costs less than the average Kentucky elementary school. We priced closure 5,832 ways using the district's own numbers. The median result loses $21,971 a year, and 55 percent of scenarios lose money.

The district needs growth, not closures. Enrollment is down 10 percent while the county's child count is flat. Costs are up at every school. The tax rate is lower than almost every neighbor's.

We ask the board for four things:

  1. Keep NMES Permanent in the facility plan.
  2. Give the school the four years until the next plan to grow, with public targets.
  3. Let the school and community raise grant and private money for the building.
  4. Say publicly that the district will help this school grow.

Written by Dr. Ryan Bradley, a former NMES King. The analysis and report writing were accelerated with the use of an AI research assistant. Built from public records and Open Records Requests only. Full report and model at the bottom of this page.

Part One

The Case Against Closing NMES

Four facts: the school works, it is not expensive, closing it frees very little, and closing it risks a lot. Most of the proof is the district's own paperwork.

Fact One

The school works.

On the state's 2024-25 tests, NMES is first in the county in every subject. It beats the state average in science and writing.

Two decades of scores

Fact Two

The school is not expensive.

On the newest state spending file (2024-25), NMES costs $17,903 per student. The average Kentucky elementary school costs $19,300. NMES runs 7 percent below the state average.

More detail: the cost record

What each elementary school has cost, every year the state published it

Two reporting eras, so compare within an era, not across the gap: 2012 to 2017 is the state's school-level spending report; 2024 and 2025 are the state's total-expenditure files, a broader measure. The 2013 NMES point includes a one-time renovation charge. The state did not publish comparable school files for the middle years; the full 25-year record is in the report. In every era the pattern is the same: NMES runs a modest premium for its size, and on the newest file it sits below the Kentucky elementary average.

More students, lower cost per student, with every new cost counted

Most of what NMES costs is the building and its fixed staff. Each added student brings real new costs, and this curve counts all of them: $700 of supplies and other costs, $500 of busing, a new teacher ($49,150) for every 16 students added beyond the 25 seats already open at the district's own class-size caps (1 per 16 is how the school actually staffed when it last ran over 200 students), and a support hire ($28,500) for every 75. Fact Three

Closing it frees very little.

Most school spending moves with the students. Teachers still teach the same kids. Supplies, books, and field trips travel too. Only the building's own costs stop, and the district's own $661,139 claim does not survive its own appendices.

The big cost numbers quoted for this school ($2.5 million federal, $1.7 million budgeted) spread district-wide costs across buildings. Central office, district buses, and the maintenance pool do not disappear when a building closes. Per-student cost is an allocation key, not a savings estimate.

The district's $661,139 claim vs the honest range

The district's response prices closure savings at "$661,138.94 MINIMUM." Hold that against the range in the model below: even our best case, with the building sold, three teachers cut at the district's own price, and not one family leaving, reaches $488,631. Their "minimum" sits 35 percent above our best case. Their own appendices explain the gap:

• $493,407 of it is staffing, and the same appendix says: "All current staff would be retained." The savings arrive only later, by attrition, if at all. Year one: $0.
• $40,693 of the expense lines travel with the students: supplies, books, field trips, printing. That money is spent wherever the kids sit.
• Not one dollar of added busing and not one leaving family appear anywhere in the 48-page response. Those are the two costs that push closures into the red.

Netted honestly, the district's own worksheet comes out $2,599 a year in the red, right beside our model's median of a $21,971 yearly loss. What survives from their sheet, about $107,039 of building costs plus $20,000 of insurance, is exactly what our model already credits. Source: Response to the 10 Questions, archived in the repository.

Fact Four

Closing it risks a lot, every year, forever.

Schools compete for families now, and every family that leaves takes about $5,100 a year of state money with it, permanently. That risk is bigger than anything a closure saves.

More detail: the market and the leak table

Schools compete now. K-5 is where you win or lose families.

Under HB 563, state money follows the child to whichever district wins the family. The district's own numbers show the business shape: it loses about $947 a year on each elementary student and makes it back in middle school (+$761) and high school (+$476). Elementary is the cost of winning a family. Grades 6-12 are the payback.

A kindergartner carries $60,000 to $77,000 of state funding over 13 years (flat to 2.1 percent yearly growth; nothing in law guarantees any increase). One in seven current middle and high schoolers came through NMES. Closing the only school on the east side of a 110-square-mile zone, with Paris Independent next door, Fayette pulling 54 commuters, and homeschool and the state virtual academy open for business, is quitting the market where the competition is thickest.

What leaving families cost

Every exit is free to the family and funded for the district that wins the child. And the first wave is only the start. The missing kids move up a grade each year, and new kindergartners follow their older siblings out. Once every grade from K through 12 is short, the yearly loss is more than double the first hit. Priced at the $4,626 base plus $500 of typical add-ons.

A "transitional" label by itself pushes families out: nobody enrolls a kindergartner in a school marked for closing.

What the district's own 48-page response shows

• Capacity changes with the audience. To justify closure, the receiving schools have "10 spare classrooms." To justify bonds, the same document's facilities study says those schools are 98 and 93 percent full, the middle school is over capacity, and NMES is the district's only school with room. NMES's own rated capacity moves between 154, 175, and 198 across their papers.
• Closing NMES fixes 9 percent of the facilities problem. The district's study lists $91.4 million of school needs. NMES is $8.5 million of it. The two schools receiving its students carry $23.2 million.
• The bond list skipped NMES entirely. $5.86 million of recent bonded projects: turf, track, scoreboards, classroom audio. NMES's only capital lines anywhere: two window repairs, $200 and $233.40.
• Admin spending: their own audit table shows +42 percent (FY23 to FY25) while their infographic says +31. Insurance up 8x, plus $111,104 of one-time payouts.

Source: Response to the 10 Questions, July 2026, archived in full in the repository.

The bottom line: grow the school, and the middle case GAINS $102,780 a year. Close it, and the middle case LOSES $21,971 a year.
Part Two

The District Needs Growth, Not Closures

The district has a real money problem. It is district-wide, NMES did not cause it, and closing NMES does not dent it. Three problems, three levers, one priced plan.

The Three Problems

Money, enrollment, revenue.

The gap is real: $2.65 million last year. The causes are district-wide: an enrollment leak worth about $2.2 million a year and the region's lowest tax posture. Closing one small school touches none of them.

More detail: the three problems, charted

1. The money problem

The General Fund ran a $2.65 million deficit last year. Reserves fall about $1.1 million a year. Pandemic aid ended (about $2.95 million a year) and state funding fell with roughly 248 fewer funded students. The district also sweeps $1.32 million a year of restricted capital money into operations. That drains the fund that should pay for buildings.

2. The enrollment problem is leakage, not demographics

The county's children never left. Census counts: 3,594 kids in 2000, 3,548 in 2020. But district enrollment fell 10.2 percent since 2014. About 450 to 550 county kids, 13 to 15 percent, are now in homeschool or private school, including 259 registered homeschoolers, up from 170 five years ago. That is $2.1 to $2.3 million a year in state funding walking past both county districts. This is a competition problem, and competition problems have competition answers.

3. The revenue problem

Bourbon taxes real estate at 52.4 cents per $100, second lowest of nine area districts. Every neighbor's rate is higher than it was fourteen years ago. Bourbon's is 5.4 percent lower, the only decline in the region, while its tax base grew 107.5 percent, second fastest of the eight area counties.

The Three Levers

Grow enrollment. Cut fixed costs. Fix the rate.

Three levers the board already owns. At their low ends, with the tax rate untouched, they are worth about $2.0 million a year. The rate restore adds up to $1.7 million more.

More detail: the levers, priced

Lever 1: Grow enrollment. It works in Kentucky, at this scale, an hour away.

Eminence Independent, a small district in Henry County, was shrinking too. It rebuilt around a distinctive program and open enrollment. It grew 37 percent in a decade. Four in ten of its students now come from other districts, each bringing state money.

Federal fall counts, indexed to 2014 = 100. Eminence: 733 to 991. Bourbon: 2,912 to 2,616. District-wide, winning back 10 to 30 percent of the leaked students is worth $1.1 to $3.3 million a year net of costs.

Lever 2: Cut fixed costs district-wide, where the real money is

Inspect every non-teaching position, district-wide. Trim through attrition, not layoffs, and never touch a classroom. Worth $340,000 to $425,000 a year at the district's own loaded costs.

Consider restructuring the administration. To be fair on the record: the district's own audit table shows the recent central-office growth came mostly from insurance, one-time payouts, and contract changes, not from new hires. A structural review is still worth $224,000 to $450,000 a year.

Smarter bus routes. Busing runs $2.7 million a year, coded to no school, and no routing study has ever been produced. A 5 to 10 percent trim is $146,000 to $291,000 a year.

Smarter energy use. Kentucky school energy contracts routinely cut utility bills 10 to 25 percent and pay for themselves. Worth $50,000 to $150,000 a year, and the district concedes an energy review is already underway.

Shared services with Paris Independent. Transport, food service, back office: $100,000 to $300,000 a year.

All five together, overlap removed: $860,000 to $1.6 million a year, counted once

These five are parts of one total, not additions on top of it. Every line is priced in the model's Alternatives tab. Nothing here closes a school or cuts a teacher.

Lever 3: Restore the tax rate to its 2018 level

The rate is simply lower. Bourbon levies 52.4 cents, second lowest of nine districts, and less than this same board levied in 2018. Meanwhile the county's tax base has more than doubled since 2012. Restoring the board's own 2018 rate, 8.9 cents, brings in up to $1.7 million a year: real resources to compete for families.

The recall record, last ten years, all eight neighboring districts: voters have turned down exactly one school tax, Bath County's building nickel, in November 2024 and again in January 2025. No neighbor has lost an operating rate like this one to a recall. Fayette's 2018 petition drive fell short of the signatures and that tax stood. Bourbon's own voters were offered the petition twice, on both facilities nickels, and never used it. A levy can be recalled by the voters it taxes. A closed school cannot be recalled by the children it displaces.

Basis: about $191,000 per cent of rate, from the district's own $7,829,060 of General Fund tax collections, under KRS 160.470; the restricted building-fund levy is not counted. The plan, priced

Run the three levers at their LOW ends, with the tax rate left alone: about $2.0 million a year ($1.11 million from enrollment plus $860,000 from costs). That covers about three quarters of the $2.65 million gap by itself. Add any part of the rate restore and the plan clears the whole gap with room to spare. It balances the budget, ends the capital sweep, and closes nothing.

The Decision

Two roads.

Close the county's best-scoring school for a median result that loses money, and tell every family watching that this district answers decline with retreat. Or grow: fill the seats, cut the real fixed costs, fix the rate, and compete for families this county never lost. The numbers are not close.

On North Middletown, Specifically

Four asks that cost the district nothing.

Permanent in the plan. Four years with public targets and the tools to hit them. Let the community fund the building. Say publicly the district will grow this school. Plus three committees to run the growth plan.

More detail: each ask and the committees

1. Keep NMES Permanent in the facility plan

A facility plan commits no money; Priority 2 exists for needs not yet funded. "Transitional" saves nothing this year either, but it blocks state facility funding and declares a phase-out. Same cost: zero. Only one keeps every door open.

2. Give the school four years, with public targets, and the tools to hit them

The facility plan runs on a four-year cycle. Set public enrollment targets with the board and let the school and community run a real push. The tools are ready: a themed academy built on the county's best record, arts (the lowest-cost differentiation there is), technology (the school qualifies for the free Amazon Future Engineer program three ways, and its elementary track covers exactly K-5), or agriculture and outdoor sciences (matched to the county's economy and the school's rural zone); a preschool-to-kindergarten funnel; after-school care; HB 563 transfers; and 259 registered homeschoolers to win back. Hit the targets and everything recovers. Miss them on a fair trial, and the next plan can revisit with evidence instead of projections.

3. Let the community fund the building

Grants, private money, donated labor and materials can carry the building's real needs at no cost to the district. The district's role is one sentence in a board motion: accept the help.

4. Say publicly that the district will grow this school

A closure cloud drives families out before any vote. A public growth commitment costs nothing and is the cheapest enrollment lever the board owns. There is no pot of money that growth would squander: if staff are reassigned, closure frees about $58,774 a year.

And ask the board to stand up three committees

The three levers in Part Two each need a group that owns it and reports to the public. 1. An enrollment growth committee, charged with growing enrollment across the whole district. 2. A fixed-cost committee, charged with inspecting every fixed cost, building by building. 3. A revenue committee, charged with inspecting the revenue options that make the district competitive. Committees cost nothing, and they turn a plan on a website into work with names on it.
