# Facebook post: the scores, and the questions for the administration

Attach `build/ksa_2025_26_change_chart.png` (1600 x 1344). Plain text below is ready to paste.

Before posting: two facts in this post are not in the state's files and come from community knowledge, the principal change and the first-year teacher in fifth grade. Be sure of both. The state's 2024-25 file shows every NMES teacher that year was experienced (0 percent of students taught by inexperienced teachers), so a first-year fifth-grade teacher in 2025-26 was a new placement; the 2025-26 report card, due later this fall, will show it in the turnover and inexperienced-teacher figures. Everything else traces to the state files archived at savenmes.org.

---

Two springs ago, North Middletown Elementary was the best elementary school in Bourbon County, and it was not close. On the state's 2023-24 scorecard NMES posted 74.5, a green rating, fourteen points clear of the next county school. It led the county in reading, math, science and social studies. In 2024-25 it was first in every subject the state scored.

This year it is last in every subject, and Kentucky has named it a Comprehensive Support and Improvement school, one of 53 in the state.

Look at the chart. Reading fell 22 points. Social studies fell 25. Writing fell 52. Nothing else in the county moved more than 15 points in either direction; Bourbon Central and Cane Ridge were mostly flat. The two largest drops anywhere in the county are in the two subjects the state tests in a single grade, the fifth.

So what changed between those two springs? Not the children. The tested classes were the same size. What changed were decisions made above the classroom. The school got a new principal. The fifth grade, the one room that carries three of the five tested subjects, got a first-year teacher. And a closure discussion hung over the building, reaching the public inside the state testing window.

Nobody should be pointing fingers at a first-year teacher or a new principal. They were put where they were put. The questions belong to the district administration, and the board should be asking them: How does the county's best elementary school become a state-identified school in two years on your watch? Who decided to staff the fifth grade that way in a closure year, and what support came with that decision? When the scores arrived, what was the plan?

From the outside, this does not look like a district where student outcomes have been the priority. It looks like a school that was allowed to slide while the conversation was about closing it.

The state's first step for a CSI school is an audit, within 45 days, of the district's own capacity to lead a turnaround. Those are the right questions. The board should not wait for Frankfort to ask them.
